Tools: TypeScript on Node.js. All project code is written in TypeScript, tests in Vitest, the ILP model is solved through the npm package highs (HiGHS) [8]. TypeScript is chosen because the solver is meant to be published openly as an online tool and/or used in an existing school platform, and in both cases JavaScript is the closest fit and the easiest to integrate.

1. Description

Schools in Kazakhstan build their weekly timetable by hand or in spreadsheets: 20–60 classes in grades 5–11, 50–100 teachers, 35–40 lessons per week, lessons taught in subgroups and two shifts. We formulate the problem as constrained optimisation in the terms of the XHSTT model for high school timetabling [1], build a generator of realistic instances from Kazakhstani curricula and sanitary rules, and implement and compare a greedy constructive heuristic based on graph colouring, simulated annealing with neighbourhoods borrowed from graph colouring, and an integer programming model as an exact reference on small instances. The outcome is a solver library and an experimental study: time to a feasible timetable at real school size and its quality in open, checkable numbers (gaps per teacher, spread of subjects, daily difficulty). The proven optimum on small instances and a lower bound on larger ones serve as a check that the method works. The library is intended for later use in a school platform that one of the authors is developing.

2. Motivation and main challenge

Timetabling is NP-hard already with class and teacher clashes alone. Real school rules add gap-free days for pupils, a weekly load limit per grade (Sanitary Rules ДСМ-76, Appendix 3 [12]), subgroup lessons that run in parallel (State Standard ГОСО, Order No. 348, §43 [14]), two shifts and teacher unavailability — so a timetable assembled by hand usually breaks several rules, and every later edit breaks others. The challenge is twofold: quickly reach a feasible timetable (zero hard violations) on instances of 40–60 classes, then improve soft quality (teacher gaps, spread of a subject over the week, an even daily difficulty according to the official subject difficulty scale) and report it in numbers that a deputy head can read and anyone can recompute.

Existing solutions

Automatic school timetabling is not new in Kazakhstan, but everywhere it remains a black box. The developer of the free electronic gradebook BilimClass (over 6,000 schools, according to the company) announced a built-in timetable constructor in August 2026: by the press release, up to 100 classes in under a minute and “at least 85 % of lessons placed without remarks” on the first run, taking clashes, rooms, subgroups and difficulty scores into account. The algorithm, cost function and quality metrics are not disclosed, the platform documentation does not describe the constructor, and there are no independent checks [15]. The free browser generator Class-KZ [16] and the desktop program “Raspisanie” by KostanaySoft [17] also place lessons automatically, but without disclosing the method or any measure of quality; the international aSc TimeTables and the Russian 1C:ASR “School” [18] formalise the norms of other countries. Academic work in Kazakhstan deals with universities and colleges [19], not schools. None of these lets an outsider check what quality the timetable actually has — the project closes exactly this gap: an open formulation with Kazakhstani norms as constraints, reproducible methods, and quality reported in absolute numbers that anyone can recompute on open instances. The proven optimum on small instances and a lower bound on class-level soft costs at any size confirm that the method works; they are a check, not the main claim.

3. Central algorithmic content

Model

Lessons are events with a class, a teacher, a subject and a number of weekly hours taken from the standard curricula (Order No. 500, Appendices 7 and 88 [13]). Hard constraints: one lesson per class per slot (except parallel subgroup lessons), one lesson per teacher per slot, every lesson placed, a class's lessons within its shift and under a daily limit derived from the weekly load, no gaps inside a class's day, teacher unavailability respected. Soft constraints, weighted: teacher gaps, the same subject twice in a day, poor spread of a subject over the week, teacher preferences, broken double lessons, an uneven number of lessons per day, and an uneven daily sum of subject difficulty scores (ДСМ-76, Appendix 4 [12]). The cost is lexicographic: hard violations first, then the weighted sum of soft ones — as in XHSTT [1]. Rooms are outside the model: choosing a room for every lesson adds a resource-assignment dimension beyond the course budget; specialised rooms (gym, laboratories) can be added later as additional clash resources.

Conflict graph and constructive heuristic

Lessons are vertices, edges join lessons sharing a class or a teacher, slots are colours. The constructive heuristic orders lessons by saturation degree (DSATUR [3]) and the “most constrained first” principle, and picks the slot with the smallest increase in soft cost.

Local search

Simulated annealing [4] over move, swap and Kempe chain neighbourhoods [5]; Kempe chains come from graph colouring and keep class and teacher clashes at zero while relocating whole chains of lessons. This combination underlies the GOAL solver, the winner of ITC 2011 [6]. Cost deltas are computed incrementally, in amortised O(1) per move, from class and teacher occupancy structures with per-day counters. Tabu search is a second metaheuristic on the same neighbourhoods, so the two can be compared directly; the parameter study covers the cooling schedule and the neighbourhood mix.

Exact reference

An integer linear model of the same problem following [7] (binary x[lesson, slot]), solved by HiGHS [8] under a time limit, gives optima on instances of 5–10 classes — this is how the gap of the heuristics is measured there. If the WASM build does not fit the time limit, the same model written to an LP file is solved by native HiGHS in a child process; CP-SAT (OR-tools) [9] is the last-resort fallback solver.

On larger instances the lower bound comes from a decomposition. The same subject twice in a day, the spread over the week, double lessons, the number of lessons per day and the daily difficulty sum depend on the timetable of one class only. The same ILP model restricted to a single class, with teacher clashes dropped, is a relaxation and is small enough to solve to optimality; the sum of these per-class minima is a valid lower bound on the total soft cost at any instance size. Teacher gaps keep a bound of zero, so they are reported in absolute numbers rather than as a gap.

Why this combination

Selection criteria: proven strength on school timetabling, few parameters, a measurable result, and enough algorithmic work of our own to fit the course budget. Pure ILP or CP-SAT on a 30-class school with soft constraints does not finish within minutes, and contains no algorithm of ours — hence it is a reference, not a method. At ITC 2011 the top places went to local search methods [2], [6], not population-based ones; the latter have more parameters and are harder to explain. A constructive heuristic without an improvement phase leaves the soft cost high, a single metaheuristic gives no comparison, and the off-the-shelf solvers KHE and FET [10], [11] carry no algorithmic content of our own. Hence the combination: DSATUR [3] as a cheap start rooted in graph colouring, SA [4] and tabu on shared neighbourhoods as two simple methods with a fair comparison, and ILP [7] via HiGHS [8] in the same Node stack as the gap measure. Caveat: this is the best choice for a course project with a measurable poster, not a provably best solver; CP-SAT hybrids may win in practice, and the gap-to-bound experiment will show where that line lies.

Data

A generator of Kazakhstani-profile instances (10/30/60/100 classes, two shifts, subgroup splitting from 24 pupils [14], curriculum hours per grade [13]) that plants a hidden feasible timetable — so every instance is guaranteed to be solvable.

Quality references

The main result is reported per instance in absolute numbers: hard violations by type, gaps per teacher per week, the share of subjects with poor spread, the spread of the daily difficulty sum. The validator computes them on open instances with fixed seeds, so anyone can rerun and recompute them. Two anchors within the core plan show that these numbers are good and not merely measured. First, our own ILP model [7]–[9]: proven optima on small instances and the decomposition lower bound at every size. Second, the generator's hidden timetable: a known feasible solution. Its soft cost is only an upper reference: it shows that the heuristics improve on a feasible start, not how far they are from the optimum. Independent external checks (the HSEval validator, the KHE and FET solvers, public instances from the HSTT archive) are listed under “If time remains” in section 4.

Experiments

Time to the first feasible timetable against instance size for greedy, SA and ILP; soft cost against time, broken down into absolute per-constraint numbers, with the decomposition lower bound; gap to the optimum on small instances; ablation of the Kempe chain neighbourhood. A separate measurement in the metrics of the industry's claimed benchmark [15]: a 100-class instance, a 60-second limit, the share of lessons placed without violations and the time to feasibility — an open method against the claimed “100 classes in a minute, at least 85 % of lessons”. This is a measurement in their metrics on an open instance, not a head-to-head comparison: their constructor also handles rooms, and our instance is solvable by construction. Fixed seeds, five repetitions, medians and spread.

Task contents

  1. Formalise the entities (classes, teachers, subjects, slots, shifts, subgroups) and all hard and soft constraints; define our own JSON format for instances and solutions that keeps the XHSTT terminology [1], with a loader and a writer. Deliverable: the data types and instance loading that every other task depends on.
  2. The generator builds a school from curriculum hours [13] and parameters (number of classes, shifts, share of classes with subgroups, teacher loads, unavailability): it first lays out a hidden feasible timetable, then derives the requirements from it, so the instance is solvable by construction. Deliverable: instance sets of 10/30/60/100 classes with fixed seeds.
  3. A function that, given an instance and a solution, counts hard violations by type and the weighted soft sum; unit tests for every constraint (a violation is caught, a correct solution passes). This is the shared “judge” for all methods.
  4. Conflict graph, DSATUR order [3], slot choice by minimum cost increase. A fast initial solution and the baseline for comparison.
  5. Local search with three neighbourhoods [4], [5], incremental cost updates, a cooling schedule and restarts. The main method of the project.
  6. A binary-variable model following [7], solved by HiGHS [8] under a time limit; the optimum on small instances as the measure of the heuristics' gap; the same model run per class gives the decomposition lower bound at any size.
  7. Rendering of the “class × day × lesson” grid with violations highlighted — for debugging and for the poster.
  8. A second metaheuristic on the same neighbourhoods with a tabu list; comparison with SA under an equal time budget.
  9. A grid over initial temperature, cooling rate and neighbourhood mix; choice of settings for the main experiments, sensitivity plots.
  10. Runs of all methods on all instances with fixed seeds and repetitions, metric collection and plots.
  11. An A0 poster to the course requirements and a short report: problem statement, methods, results, conclusions.

Risks and fallbacks

RiskFallback
Full XHSTT is far larger than our model: 16 constraint types, each with its own deviation and cost function, plus event splitting and resource assignment. The core uses our own format with XHSTT terminology, so no external tool can read our instances directly. The XML export is the first optional step and covers only the constraints our instances use.
The even daily sum of difficulty scores appears to have no direct counterpart among the standard XHSTT constraints. It is computed by our validator as an extension of the format, so an external validator cannot confirm this part of the cost.
The full ILP model gives no useful lower bound on medium and large instances: the LP relaxation of idle-time and spread constraints is known to be loose. The gap to the optimum is claimed only where the optimum is proven. On larger instances the bound is the sum of per-class minima; it ignores teacher coupling, so it may stay loose, and teacher gaps are reported in absolute numbers.
The WASM build of HiGHS is too slow even on small instances. The same model as an LP file solved by native HiGHS; CP-SAT [9] as the last resort.

If time remains

External comparisons are not part of the 74 hours. They are added in this order; the XML export comes first because every later step needs it:

  1. Export of our instances and solutions to XHSTT XML [1] for the constraints we use (time assignment, clashes, unavailability, idle times, spread, busy-time limits).
  2. HSEval [10]: recompute the cost of our solutions on our own instances by the XHSTT standard, independently of our code (without the difficulty term) — a guard against errors in our cost function.
  3. KHE [10] on our instances exported to XHSTT, under an equal time budget and by the number of evaluated moves, since KHE is written in C.
  4. FET [11] as a check that a feasible timetable is attainable; its cost model is its own, so the comparison is on hard constraints only.
  5. Two or three public instances from the HSTT archive [1] against best-known results. This requires exact support of every constraint the chosen instance uses, including event splitting.
